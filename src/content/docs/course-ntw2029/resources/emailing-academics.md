---
title: Emailing academics about their work
---

If you have a question about a source you're writing about, you can email its author. Academics sometimes answer students' questions, and you're more likely to receive a reply if your email is easy to read and easy to answer. This page explains how to write that email. [:Which author should I email?](#x-which-author-should-i-email)

## Guidelines

### Before writing

1. Understand the purpose of your email. You are asking for clarification, reasoning, or information. Some academics enjoy a longer exchange, but your first email shouldn't ask for or expect one.

2. Have a genuine reason for writing. Read the relevant publication carefully, along with the author's response and other commentaries if there are any, and make sure your question isn't already answered there.

3. You can disagree with the academic you're writing to. It's perfectly acceptable to question their reasoning or challenge their conclusions. Respectful disagreement means explaining your concern clearly and asking a question without assuming that you are necessarily right and the author is wrong.

4. Choose one focused question. Identify the question that matters most to your understanding. Don't send a long list of questions or ask the academic to evaluate your writing.

### Writing the email

5. Write a specific subject line. Name the publication you're asking about, e.g. _Question about your article "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation"_.

6. Explain your thinking, but only as much as necessary. It is often useful to explain how you interpreted a passage or why you question a claim. However, you don't need to reproduce the entire reasoning process that led you to your question.

7. Make it easy to respond. The academic should be able to understand your question without having to reread several publications, and ideally be able to answer it in a few sentences. Remember that you are asking someone to do you a favor.

8. Keep your email short. Aim for approximately 150-250 words. Briefly introduce yourself, identify the publication you're asking about, provide any necessary context, and get to your question.

9. Be respectful without being excessively formal. Use an appropriate greeting, straightforward language, and a brief expression of thanks. Avoid elaborate flattery, repeated apologies, unnecessarily complicated sentences, or excessive deference.

### After sending

10. Don't expect a rapid reply, or any reply at all. Academics have many competing demands on their time, and some receive large amounts of correspondence. Plan your paper so that it doesn't depend on an answer. A lack of response doesn't necessarily mean your question was inappropriate or uninteresting.

1. If you do get a reply, you can cite it in your paper if needed to support your claims (but don't count it as one of your required academic sources). [:how to cite?](#x-how-to-cite-a-personal-communication)

## Examples

The two emails below are invented. The article, the commentary, and the people named in them don't exist.

### Example to model

This email follows the guidelines above. It identifies the publication, explains the writer's reasoning only as far as the question requires, and asks one question that can be answered in a few sentences.

_Subject: Question about your article "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation"_

_Dear Professor Mitchell,_

_I am a student at the National University of Singapore, currently studying evolutionary explanations of human cooperation. I recently read your 2021 target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation," along with the published commentaries and your response._

_I was particularly interested in Chen and Roberts' commentary, "The Limits of Reciprocity in Large Societies." They question whether your explanation applies to modern societies, where individuals frequently cooperate with strangers whom they are unlikely to encounter again. My understanding is that the expectation of future interactions plays an important role in making cooperation advantageous under your model._

_I noticed that your response addresses several other criticisms raised by Chen and Roberts, but not this particular concern. It seems to me that this concern is a central challenge to your explanation, since cooperation remains widespread even in societies with millions of people._

_Does your model account for cooperation between individuals who are unlikely to interact again, or do you see this as a limitation of the explanation?_

_Thank you for any clarification you might be able to offer._

_Best regards,_\
_Daniel_\
_National University of Singapore_

### Example to avoid

The writer's questions are reasonable ones and demonstrate genuine intellectual engagement with the articles. The email's length is also reasonable. Yet there are several problems with it:

- It raises several overlapping questions about reciprocity (what the term covers, whether it includes indirect reciprocity, whether reputation is the real explanation) without settling on one (guideline 4).
- While it's fine to ask a very specific question, the email asks the academic to explain the disagreement between the article and the commentary in a general sense, instead of narrowing it to a point the academic could answer briefly (guidelines 4 and 7).
- It asks whether game theory is a better explanation than cultural evolution, a question that would take an essay to answer (guidelines 1 and 7).
- It asks the academic to evaluate the writer's thesis and choose evidence for it (guideline 4).
- It asks the author to justify not responding to the commentary ("Can you tell me why?") instead of asking about the issue itself, which can put the author on the defensive (guideline 3).
- Taken together, "Hi Prof Mitchell," "undergrad at NUS," "it'd be awesome," "It would be great if you could clear that up" and "Cheers" make the tone too casual for a first email to an academic you don't know, even though any one of them might be fine later in an exchange (guideline 9). Also, a non-Singaporean reader may not know what NUS stands for.

_Hi Prof Mitchell, I'm Daniel, an undergrad at NUS. I've been reading your paper, "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation," and the commentary by Chen and Roberts, "The Limits of Reciprocity in Large Societies." It's a really interesting debate!_

_One thing I'm curious about is what exactly you mean by reciprocal cooperation. Does it only apply to situations where people cooperate because they expect to see each other again? Because if that's the case, I don't really see how it works in modern societies where most people are strangers. The commentary challenges you on this, but you don't respond, even though you respond to their other criticisms. Can you tell me why? Also, are you including indirect reciprocity also, where people cooperate because of their reputation? Then again, wouldn't that make reputation the explanation rather than reciprocity? And Chen and Roberts seem to be making a different distinction, so I'm not totally sure what the disagreement is really about. It would be great if you could clear that up!_

_I'm also curious whether you think game theory is actually a better explanation than cultural evolution. They both seem pretty convincing to me, so I'd love to know which one you think has stronger evidence behind it._

_Anyway, here's the thesis I'm working with for my assignment: Game theory helps explain how cooperation first evolved in small communities, but cultural institutions are more important for maintaining cooperation in large modern societies. Do you agree with that? I'm still figuring out my argument, so it'd be awesome if you could let me know whether you think it holds up and what evidence I should use to support it._

_Thanks a lot! Looking forward to hearing what you think._

_Cheers,_\
_Daniel_

##### :x which author should i email

Most articles with several authors mark one of them as the corresponding author, usually on the first page beside an email address. If there is one, write to that person.

Some articles don't mark a corresponding author. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_ target articles and commentaries, for example, list every author's email address instead. In that case, I recommend writing to the first author alone, who usually did the most work on the paper. And if the first author's email address no longer works, try the last author, who (in a scientific paper) is usually the senior researcher running the lab that produced the paper.

Don't write more than one author unless you know your email didn't reach the recipient.

##### :x how to cite a personal communication

APA style treats an email as a personal communication. Because readers can't retrieve it, a personal communication is cited in the text only and doesn't go in your reference list. Give the person's initials and surname, the words "personal communication," and the exact date, in one of these formats:

- Narrative citation: F. M. Lastname (personal communication, Month Day, Year), e.g. _J. Mitchell (personal communication, March 3, 2026) explained that the model was not intended to cover cooperation between strangers._
- Parenthetical citation: (F. M. Lastname, personal communication, Month Day, Year), e.g. _The model was not intended to cover cooperation between strangers (J. Mitchell, personal communication, March 3, 2026)._

Unlike the course's other citations, a personal communication citation has no page number.

A reply tells you what the author meant, but it hasn't been through peer review, so treat it as a clarification of the published work rather than as evidence in its own right.
