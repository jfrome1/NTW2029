## Writing to an academic about their research

### Before writing

1. Understand the purpose of your email. You are asking for clarification, reasoning, or information, not inviting the scholar into an extended discussion or debate.

2. Have a genuine reason for writing. Read the relevant publication carefully, and make sure your question isn't already answered in the text.

3. Don't be afraid to disagree. It's perfectly acceptable to question a scholar's reasoning or challenge their conclusions. Respectful disagreement means explaining your concern clearly and asking a question without assuming that you are necessarily right and the author is wrong.

4. Choose one focused question. Identify the question that matters most to your understanding. Don't send a long list of questions or ask the author to evaluate your entire argument.

### Writing the email

5. Explain your thinking, but only as much as necessary. It is often useful to explain how you interpreted a passage or why you question a claim. However, you don't need to reproduce the entire reasoning process that led you to your question.

6. Make it easy to respond. The recipient should be able to understand your question without having to reread several publications, and ideally be able to answer it in a few sentences. Remember that you are asking someone to do you a favor.

7. Keep your email short. Aim for approximately 150-250 words. Briefly introduce yourself, identify the publication you're asking about, provide any necessary context, and get to your question.

8. Be respectful without being excessively formal. Use an appropriate greeting, straightforward language, and a brief expression of thanks. Avoid elaborate flattery, repeated apologies, unnecessarily complicated sentences, or excessive deference.

### After sending

9. Don't assume you'll receive a reply. Scholars have many competing demands on their time, and some receive large amounts of correspondence. A lack of response doesn't necessarily mean your question was inappropriate or uninteresting.

## Examples

### Example to avoid

Note that the writer has genuine intellectual curiosity and potentially worthwhile questions. The problems are with the email's length, organization, focus, tone, and demands on the recipient.

Dear Professor Mitchell,

I am Daniel, an undergraduate student at the National University of Singapore. I hope you are doing well! I am currently studying evolutionary explanations of human cooperation and came across your target article, "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation," as well as the commentary by Chen and Roberts, "The Limits of Reciprocity in Large Societies." I am referencing both papers in my research to better understand how game theory can explain the development and continuation of cooperation, particularly in relation to the disagreements between the different explanations.

While reading your article and the subsequent responses to the commentaries, I noticed that Chen and Roberts challenge the applicability of your model to large societies where individuals may not encounter each other repeatedly. I was especially interested in this point as it seems to question whether the conditions necessary for cooperation in your model are actually present in modern human societies. I have a few questions regarding your reasoning and the expectations of the model, which I hope you might be able to clarify so that I can better understand the extent to which your explanation is applicable.

From my understanding, you argue that cooperation can be established through game-theoretic mechanisms because individuals who cooperate may receive greater benefits in future interactions compared to those who choose not to cooperate. In your article, you state that "the expectation of future encounters can transform cooperation into a strategy that is advantageous even among self-interested individuals" (p. 18).

I was wondering whether this means that the possibility of repeated interactions is a necessary condition for your explanation to work, or whether it is simply one condition that makes cooperation more likely. If repeated interactions are not necessary, what would motivate individuals to cooperate when they have no expectation of interacting with the same person again?

In Chen and Roberts' commentary, they argue that "the conditions supporting reciprocity in small groups cannot be assumed to operate in populations where most interactions occur between strangers" (p. 42). While I understand that game theory can demonstrate how cooperation is beneficial when certain conditions are fulfilled, I was wondering whether this actually provides evidence that these conditions were responsible for cooperation evolving, rather than just explaining how cooperation could occur if individuals were already inclined to cooperate.

In your response to Chen and Roberts, you address their concerns regarding the assumption of rational decision-making and whether cooperation can initially emerge among individuals who would otherwise benefit from defection. However, I noticed that you did not directly address their point about large societies. I personally think that this is one of their stronger criticisms, since it questions the extent to which the explanation can be applied beyond smaller populations, which seems important if the theory is intended to explain human cooperation more generally.

Would you say that your responses to their other criticisms are also sufficient to address this concern, or is there another reason why this particular point was not discussed? I was also wondering whether you consider the criticism to be a challenge to the theory itself or only to its application in certain environments.

Another point I wanted to clarify is the distinction between cooperation being established and cooperation continuing once it has been established. You mention that "once cooperative strategies become established, the benefits of mutual cooperation can support their continued stability" (p. 21). However, I am not entirely sure whether the conditions which allow cooperation to initially develop are necessarily the same as those which allow it to continue, especially when the population becomes much larger and the likelihood of meeting the same person again decreases.

While I definitely agree that cooperation may be beneficial to individuals even in large societies, it seems that this does not necessarily explain why individuals would cooperate in situations where defection could provide greater immediate benefits without future consequences. Would this suggest that the model is more applicable to explaining how cooperation initially evolved rather than why cooperation continues to exist today, or would you argue that these are not necessarily different questions?

In fact, it is possible that institutions, social norms and reputational systems could also provide incentives for cooperation even when direct reciprocity is unlikely. For example, individuals may cooperate with strangers because they expect that failing to cooperate would lead to negative consequences from other members of society. Yet, these explanations seem to rely on additional mechanisms which may not have been present when cooperation first evolved. I would argue that this could mean that the original explanation is still valid, but that it is not sufficient in explaining the continuation of cooperation in modern societies. What are your thoughts on this qualification? Would you still consider your model a general explanation of cooperation if it requires other mechanisms to explain its maintenance?

I was also wondering what evidence you would personally consider sufficient to challenge the game-theoretic explanation, since it seems difficult to identify situations where cooperation cannot be explained by some form of future benefit. If cooperation between strangers can be explained through indirect reciprocity or reputation, while cooperation between familiar individuals is explained through repeated interactions, it seems that the explanation could potentially account for many different situations regardless of the conditions involved. Of course, I acknowledge that this does not necessarily mean the theory is incorrect, since different mechanisms could all contribute to cooperation, but I am unsure how we would determine whether the theory has actually explained the behaviour rather than simply being consistent with it.

With all this said, may I clarify whether you believe that Chen and Roberts' argument regarding large societies represents a significant challenge to your explanation, especially considering that it was not directly addressed in your response? Or would you say that the distinction between the emergence of cooperation and its continued maintenance allows the theory to remain applicable even if repeated interactions are less common in modern societies?

Thank you very much for taking the time to consider these questions. I understand that this is quite a lengthy email, and I greatly appreciate your willingness to read through the different concerns I have raised. I would be very grateful if you could provide your perspective on these issues, as I believe it would help me better understand the disagreement between your article and the commentary, and whether the explanations offered are sufficient for accounting for cooperation across different human societies. If there are other arguments or developments in your research that you think would be relevant to these questions, I would also be interested in hearing about them. Thank you again for your time, and I hope to hear from you!

Best Regards,
Daniel

Undergraduate Student
National University of Singapore

### Example to model:

Dear Professor Mitchell,

I am a student at the National University of Singapore, currently studying evolutionary explanations of human cooperation. I recently read your target article, "Game Theory and the Evolution of Human Cooperation," along with the published commentaries and your response.

I was particularly interested in Chen and Roberts' commentary, "The Limits of Reciprocity in Large Societies." They question whether your explanation applies to modern societies, where individuals frequently cooperate with strangers whom they are unlikely to encounter again. My understanding is that the expectation of future interactions plays an important role in making cooperation advantageous under your model.

I noticed that your response addresses several other criticisms raised by Chen and Roberts, but not this particular concern. It seems to me that this is a central challenge to your explanation, since cooperation remains widespread even in societies with millions of people.

Does your model account for cooperation between individuals who are unlikely to interact again, or do you see this as a limitation of the explanation?

Thank you for considering my question. I would appreciate any clarification you might be able to offer.

Best regards,
Daniel
National University of Singapore
