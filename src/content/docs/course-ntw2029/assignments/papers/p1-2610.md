## Purpose

This assignment will develop your ability to explain complex academic arguments clearly and accurately. You'll practice the writing skills, discussed in class, that are essential for participating in academic conversations. These skills include:

- Explaining a source more clearly than the original
- Summarizing accurately and charitably
- Writing for a generalist reader
- Clear attribution
- Using quotations effectively
- Consistent key terms
- Functional introductions

## Overview

An expository paper explains someone else's argument in a way that is clearer and simpler than the original. Write a 1250-1750 word expository paper explaining arguments from Pinker's chapter "The Meaning of Life" about why humans enjoy art, both in general, and as the argument applies to a specific art form. Write for a generalist, educated audience who hasn't read Pinker or taken this course.

## Requirements

### Structure

- **Introduction**: 1-2 paragraphs; we will discuss introduction structure in class
- **Thesis**: see the Thesis section below
- **Body Part 1**: Explain Pinker's general argument
- **Body Part 2**: Explain 1-2 specific mechanisms for your chosen art form
- **Conclusion**: Brief restatement of main points

I separated these for clarity, but don't use headers or subsections in your paper.

### Thesis

This is an expository paper, so your thesis states Pinker's argument rather than a claim of your own. Write it in one or two sentences on this pattern: "Pinker argues that people enjoy art because [his general answer]. In particular, he argues that people enjoy [your art form] because [the one or two mechanisms you explain]."

- Highlight the thesis in yellow.
- Keep verbs like "explore", "discuss", and "deconstruct" out of the thesis, since they describe what you are doing rather than what Pinker argues.

- A strong thesis for this paper does these things

### Scope

The base expectation is clearly explaining Pinker's argument. Students whose explanations are in good shape by the conference may be invited to add analysis identifying ambiguities or limitations in Pinker's argument. Analysis is an optional "stretch goal" that should only be attempted if feedback indicates the explanation is quite far along.

Explain Pinker's argument about why people enjoy art, not [:other things he also writes about](#x-other-things-he-also-writes-about). Present an argument focused on that question rather than a general summary of his article as a whole.

Explain Pinker's theories. Do not evaluate them or offer your own theories about why we enjoy art.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many quotations should I use?

There's no magic number. Use quotes when: 1) establishing credibility that you've read carefully, 2) capturing Pinker's specific terminology before explaining it, 3) providing evidence for your interpretation. Most paragraphs might have 1-2 short quotes or quoted phrases. Never rely on quotations to do your explaining for you.

### What if Pinker's argument doesn't make sense to me?

Start charitably; assume it makes sense, and re-read, closely parsing the language to infer implicit assumptions and reasons. If confusion persists after multiple readings, you might have found a genuine ambiguity or gap (and those do exist). Explain the argument, explicitly noting any gaps or limitations, and if the explanation is solid by the conference, I'll invite you to analyze the argument, where you'll articulate exactly what's unclear and why it matters.

##### :x What counts as explaining

Explaining means giving Pinker's reasoning, not just his conclusions. To explain, you must be clearer than Pinker ([:how?](#x-how-to-be-clearer-than-pinker)), and you must avoid circular reasoning ([:how?](#x-how-to-avoid-circular-reasoning)).

Explanation also means that you cover all of the steps in the argument, so the reader understands everything they need to know for the argument to make sense beginning to end (or, if there are gaps, they are explicitly noted for the reader).

##### :x how-to-be-clearer-than-pinker

Pinker uses figurative language that evokes meaning without fully explaining its meaning. If you quote or use his phrases, you must clearly explain their meaning in evolutionary terms:

- "pleasure circuits" / "pleasure buttons"
- "pleasure technology"
- "mental machinery" / "mental faculties"
- "taps into the system"
- "pips of microsatisfaction"
- "auditory cheesecake"
- "tickle the sensitive spots"
- "defeat the locks"

Using Pinker's words without explanation comes across as the writer quoting Pinker because they don't fully understand him.

##### :x how-to-avoid-circular-reasoning

A common problem students face is assuming that sentences provide explanations just because they are in explanatory form ("X happens because of Y"). That's not always the case. To check, you must consider if X and Y are different enough that Y actually explains X rather than just restating it in different words.

- Circular: "We enjoy art because it gives us pleasure"
- Circular: "Art is enjoyable because it activates pleasure circuits"
- Circular: "We like beautiful things because beauty appeals to us"
- Explanatory: "We enjoy symmetrical faces because our brains evolved to use symmetry as a signal of genetic health when choosing mates"

##### :x why not write about these

Historically, students have had trouble writing effective papers on these parts of the article. I'm trying to save you frustration. There are plenty of other good choices.

##### :x why-only-one

You are limited to one or two arguments about one art form so you can explain them in depth.

##### :x What terms will my audience know?

For this assignment assume your audience understands these basic terms and concepts: evolution, species, organism, trait, natural selection

You should _briefly_ explain the following terms if you use them ([:how?](#x-how-to-briefly-explain-a-term)): Environment of Evolutionary Adaptation (EEA), reproductive fitness, adaptation, byproduct

##### :x how-to-briefly-explain-a-term

A brief explanation can be just one phrase in a sentence:

- Explaining "non-kin": "Pinker says that altruism and cooperation eventually extended to non-kin (i.e., those with no familial or genetic relationship)."
- Explaining "social bonding": "Monkeys enhance social bonding, understood as their affinity for other monkeys, by grooming each other's fur."

##### :x How to distinguish examples

Pinker's ideas:

- "Pinker argues that..."
- "He reasons that..."

Your ideas:

- "This concept is unclear..."
- "Although Pinker doesn't explain the connection, one argument supporting it is..."

Pinker's examples:

- "Pinker illustrates this idea with the example of..."
- "Pinker gives the example of..."

Your examples:

- "For instance, consider..."
- "To illustrate this, imagine..."

##### :x Examples of weak vs strong theses

- Weak thesis 1: "This paper will discuss Boyd's arguments about cooperation."
- Weak thesis 2: "Boyd has several arguments about how cooperation evolved."
- Weak thesis 3: "Boyd says that cooperation evolved and that reciprocal altruism is one reason."

Weak theses 1 and 2 describe Boyd's writing moves (what topics he discusses) rather than stating what he says about those topics. Weak thesis 3 mentions one reason supporting his conclusion, but doesn't preview how the named mechanism works.

- Strong: "Boyd states that repeated interactions between people allowed the evolution of cooperation between non-kin. These repeated interactions, he argues, allowed an altruism strategy to enhance fitness more than a strategy of defection."

The strong thesis states Boyd's central claim (not just that he has one) and previews the specific mechanism the paper will explain.

##### :x Other things he also writes about

Avoid discussing these topics, which are not strongly related to Pinker's argument about why people enjoy art:

- Why the psychology of the arts is obscure
- How art is related to status
- Why faculties of the arts are elitist
- Why people think about philosophy and religion
