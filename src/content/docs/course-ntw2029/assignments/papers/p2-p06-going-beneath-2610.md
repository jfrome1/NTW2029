### 2. Part C: The Implicit Disagreement

<!-- Next, you'll try to find "Part C": the implicit disagreement. -->

Part C is the implicit disagreement: a difference that explains why their positions differ, usually a difference in assumptions, definitions, methodological commitments, or evidentiary standards (these are examples; you might find other differences). It is implicit because neither author names it as the reason they disagree, even though each may state their own position on it. Identifying the implicit disagreement is your analytical contribution.

Differences might be:

- Theoretical: how each author frames or studies the phenomenon, what each thinks is worth knowing and why, what each thinks a good theory or explanation should do, etc.
- Empirical: what each author thinks the evidence shows, what evidence counts as sufficient, or how the data should be interpreted, etc.

Write Part C in this form (you can alter the wording, but keep the basic structure):

- Template: "They differ on [describe the assumption, definition, methodological commitment, or evidentiary standard]. [commentary] holds [its position], while [target] holds [its position]."

Part C will likely take several passes to find, so work through the how-to and the examples before you write it.

### 4. Working Thesis

A "working thesis" just means a thesis that's not finalized that you use to start writing a draft.

Combine Parts A, B, and C into one or two sentences:

- Without Part A: "They disagree about [Part B] because [Part C]."
- With Part A: "The commentary reads the target as claiming [Part A, the commentary's version], which the target does not claim. Once the target's actual claim is on the table, they still disagree about [Part B] because [Part C]."

Don't argue that one scholar is right and the other wrong; explain what drives the disagreement.

Expect your thesis to change: P05 Question 13 names the explicit disagreement, P06 names the implicit one, P07 deepens it and adds significance.

### 5. Beginning Significance

You'll develop significance more fully in P07, but beginning this thinking now will strengthen your analysis.

2. Name a candidate: write a few sentences naming something unstated that the two authors differ on, and explain the difference in their positions.

3. Check that it's implicit. If either article names this difference as the reason they disagree, that's useful information: it means you need to dig deeper. What enables that explicit disagreement? What would both authors need to accept for their positions to be compatible?

4. Check that this particular implicit difference is the basis for the explicit difference you found. Figure out how each author's position on this difference would lead to the explicit difference in Part B. If you can show that, your candidate produces the explicit disagreement, and what you've just worked out is your explanation for requirement 3. If you can't get from your candidate to Part B, it might be a real difference between the authors, but it's not the one underneath the explicit disagreement. Go back to the diagnostic questions.

5. When a candidate passes both checks, write it as Part C in the template.

##### :x Pattern 1: Claims don't match evidence

- Does the commentary show the target's prediction failed?
- Are there counterexamples to the target's universal claim?
- Does the target cherry-pick supporting cases while ignoring contradictory evidence?
- _Example: Verpooten & Eens argue that singing doesn't correlate with social complexity as Savage et al. predict._

##### :x Pattern 2: Missing important factors

- What does the commentary say the target overlooked?
- Why does that missing factor matter for understanding the phenomenon?
- _Example: Goldstein argues that theatre lacks the representational distance that Menninghaus et al.'s theory requires._

##### :x Pattern 3: Definitions don't match

- Do target and commentary define key terms differently?
- If they used the commentary's definition, would the disagreement dissolve or remain?
- _Example: Konečni argues that what Menninghaus et al. call readers' "negative emotions" are just shallow quasi-emotions, not genuine emotions._

##### :x Pattern 4: Wrong question or framework

- Does the commentary say the target asked the wrong question?
- Does the commentary claim the target's approach can't work for this type of phenomenon?
- _Example (invented commentary): Reyes & Thornton argue that Dubourg & Baumard ask the wrong question; what needs explaining is not why imaginary worlds appeal to individuals but why audiences form identities as fans of particular imaginary worlds rather than others._

##### :x Pattern 5: Logical problems or scientific standards

- Does the commentary say the target's argument is circular or logically invalid?
- Does the commentary say the theory is too vague to test or makes no testable predictions?
- Does the commentary challenge what evidence threshold is needed (burden of proof, parsimony, defaults)?
- _Example (invented commentary): Adeyemi argues that Mehr et al.'s theory is too loosely defined to test, since any observable behavior, musical or not, can be read as credibly signaling something._

**Example 1: the commentary misreads the target and disagrees about whether the target is focused on the right phenomenon**

Context: Weisberg & Friend are interested in non-fiction artworks, like documentaries, and say that Menninghaus's DEM model doesn't explain why viewers would watch non-fiction that causes negative emotions.

- Part A (commentary's description of target): _Weisberg & Friend say Menninghaus et al. claim that fiction schema is "crucial" to distancing and "primarily responsible" for it. Menninghaus et al. actually say that none of the three schemas are "mandatory for all arts" and that fiction is just one "dimension of distancing"._
- Part B (explicit disagreement): _Menninghaus et al. say the Distancing factor "consists of" the art, representation, and fiction schemas, and that for nonfiction cases where these don't apply, audiences are still distanced in space and time. Weisberg & Friend say that the DEM model doesn't explain why people watch nonfiction works that evoke negative emotions, since its main Distancing schemas don't apply._
- Part C (implicit disagreement): _The articles differ on what makes a model like the DEM a sufficient explanation; Weisberg & Friend think that any model of art reception should provide an equally persuasive account of fiction and nonfiction; Menninghaus et al. think that it's enough to account for fictional artworks and to note that the model might need some small refinements to explain non-fiction._
- How Part C was found: by asking why the factor the commentary says is missing (nonfiction) matters to Weisberg & Friend. It matters because they hold that a model of art reception has to cover fiction and nonfiction equally, a standard Menninghaus et al. don't share.
- Thesis: _Weisberg & Friend read Menninghaus et al. as claiming that the fiction schema is central to the distancing required to enjoy artworks that evoke negative emotions. Yet Menninghaus et al. do not make this claim; they say the three schemata are "not mandatory for all arts". Nonetheless, Weisberg & Friend's criticism exposes a genuine conceptual gap in the DEM: although the DEM acknowledges non-fiction distancing, the DEM does not adequately account for how non-fiction distancing occurs, thus blocking the explanation of how non-fiction can be enjoyed when it evokes negative emotions._

**Example 2: the commentary misreads the target and proposes an alternate theory**

Context: Okafor & Lindqvist (an imagined commentary, not a real one) think that social bonding is caused by people doing things in a synchronized way (i.e. synchrony), and that Savage et al. are too narrow in thinking that music evolved for social bonding.

- Part A: _Okafor & Lindqvist read Savage et al. as claiming that the pitch and melody of music are what causes social bonding. Savage et al. do mention these elements, but also state that coordinated group action is centrally important, arguing that the design features of musicality "function mechanistically to enhance predictability, aiding synchronization and harmonization when multiple people sing, dance, and play instruments together"._
- Part B: _Savage et al. say the need for social bonding in larger groups was fulfilled by musicality, "a flexible toolkit for bonding" that works "on a scale greater than" grooming and laughter and "in a variety of situations where language is less effective"; Okafor & Lindqvist say social bonding in large groups is due to synchrony across many activities other than music, including marching, chanting, and dancing._
- Part C: _They differ on whether a trait can be an adaptation for a function that other behaviors also serve: for Okafor & Lindqvist, if any synchronized activity bonds as well as music does, that means that music was not an adaptation selected for bonding; for Savage et al., it's fair to say that music is an adaptation for bonding as long as it serves that function in some ways or contexts that other activities cannot._
- How Part C was found: by asking why the commentary's alternative (synchrony in any activity) would count against Savage et al. It counts only if a trait can't be an adaptation for a function that other behaviors also serve, an assumption Savage et al. reject.
- Thesis: _Okafor & Lindqvist read Savage et al. as claiming that the pitch and melody of music are what causes social bonding. Savage et al. do mention these elements, but also state that coordinated group action is centrally important, arguing that the design features of musicality "function mechanistically to enhance predictability, aiding synchronization and harmonization when multiple people sing, dance, and play instruments together". Yet, even when Savage et al.'s claim is clarified, the two still disagree about whether musicality was selected for bonding or is just one vehicle for a general capacity to act in synchrony, because they differ on whether a trait can be an adaptation for a function other behaviors also serve._

**Example 3: the commentary reads the target accurately, but they still disagree**

Context: Verpooten & Eens say that Savage et al.'s theory predicts that more musical species should have more complex social bonds. The commentary says that species with more complex social bonds use "learned contact calls" (short, simple vocalizations) and species with more music-like communications (long, complex, varied, repeated notes) have simpler social bonds. They think this undermines Savage et al.'s theory.

- Part A: None. Verpooten & Eens quote the target's prediction as the target states it, that "across species, production or proficiency in 'musical' behaviors should predict both the number and complexity of social bonds", and Savage et al.'s response accepts the commentary as a test of that prediction.
- Part B: _Savage et al. predict that musical behaviors should track social complexity across species; Verpooten & Eens find that learned contact calls track social complexity and songs do not, and that contact calls "are not particularly music-like"._
- Part C: _They differ on what makes an animal vocalization musical for the purposes of the prediction: for Verpooten & Eens, music requires song-like elements such as complex, ordered, and repeated notes; for Savage et al., learned contact calls should count as music._
- How Part C was found: by asking whether the two define a key term differently. They do: the prediction's test depends on what counts as musical, and the commentary and the target draw that line in different places.
- Thesis: _Verpooten & Eens disagree with Savage et al. about the implications of cross-species evidence about vocalization because they differ on what types of animal vocalizations count as musical: for Verpooten & Eens, animal songs, not learned contact calls, count as music, and song does not track social complexity; for Savage et al., both songs and calls count as music, so they think the data supports their theory rather than undermining it._
