## Purpose

This assignment helps you select a commentary, understand its criticism, and check whether the commentary represents the target accurately before you build any analysis on it. By the end, you will know one issue the two articles explicitly disagree about and whether that disagreement is substantial enough to carry a paper.

## Overview

Select a commentary that criticizes your target article (recommendations below), then answer the numbered questions that help you identify the commentary's criticism and verify whether the commentary accurately describes the target. Avoid "picking a side", and don't try to determine your thesis yet (you'll do that in P06).

Each question has a general length limit. Stay within those limits: deciding what to leave out is part of the work.

## Requirements

Copy and paste the questions below into a document and type your answer below each question. Keep the questions numbered and keep each answer as its own paragraph or paragraphs under its question, so that every question has a visible answer.

Each quotation is one, two, or three complete sentences, with the page number in parentheses after it. A page number in parentheses is the only citation this assignment needs; you don't need APA in-text citations or a reference list. <span class="smaller-text"> [:Why do I need both a paraphrase and a quotation?](#x-why-do-i-need-both-a-paraphrase-and-a-quotation) </span>

Label each quotation when you first quote it: C1, C2, and so on for the commentary, T1, T2, and so on for the target, and R1, R2, and so on for the authors' response. After that, refer to a quotation by its label instead of quoting it again in full. When you need to analyze particular wording, quote just those few words and give the label.

### Part 1: Select Your Commentary

Choose one commentary from the recommended list below (or [:propose an alternative](#x-proposing-an-alternative-commentary)). Read the full commentary, not just the abstract.

## Recommended Commentaries

### Target Article 1: Dubourg & Baumard (2022) - Why Imaginary Worlds?

_Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 45, e276: 1-72

- Main article: pp. 1-18
- Author's response: p. 63

**Recommended commentaries:**

Dunk, R., & Mar, R. A. (2022). Cognitive exploration drives engagement and re-engagement with imaginary worlds, but not spatial exploration as predicted by evolutionary theory. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _45_, e281. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X21002168

> _Abstract:_ The empirical evidence for exploration underlying the appeal of imaginary worlds is mostly absent or contradictory. Openness, and the cognitive exploration it represents, provides a better account than the overall drive to explore predicted by evolutionary theory. Furthermore, exploration cannot explain why imaginary worlds foster frequent re-engagement.

---

Moore, R., & Hills, T. (2022). The evolution of imagination and the adaptive value of imaginary worlds. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _45_, e288. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X2100217X

> _Abstract:_ Characterizing the cultural evolution of imaginary worlds as a hedonic but non-adaptive exaptation from evolved exploratory tendencies, Dubourg and Baumard defend too narrow a conception of the adaptive evolution of imaginary worlds. Imagination and its imaginary worlds are ancient and adaptive, allowing deliberation over actions, consequences, and futures worth aspiring to, often engendering the world we see around us.

---

Norman, K. E., & Goldstein, T. R. (2022). All non-real worlds provide exploration: Evidence from developmental psychology. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _45_, e290. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X21002314

> _Abstract:_ While Dubourg and Baumard argue that predisposition toward exploration draws us to fictional environments, they fail to answer their titular question: "Why Imaginary Worlds?" Research in pretend play, psychological distancing, and theatre shows that being "imaginary" (i.e., any type of unreal, rather than only fantastically unreal) makes exploration of any fictional world profoundly different than that of real-life unfamiliar environments.

---

Nyhout, A., & Lee, R. (2022). Young children are not driven to explore imaginary worlds. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _45_, e291. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X2100234X

> _Abstract:_ We address Dubourg and Baumard's claim that imaginary worlds are most appealing early in the lifespan when the exploratory drive is highest. Preschool-age children prefer fictions set in the real world, and fantastical information can be difficult for children to represent in real time. We speculate that a drive to explore imaginary worlds may emerge after children acquire substantial real-world skills and knowledge. An account of age effects on fictional preferences should encompass developmental change.

---

Shtulman, A. (2022). The familiar appeal of imaginary worlds. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _45_, e298. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X21002338

> _Abstract:_ Imaginary worlds may satisfy our need to explore, but it's an open question what we are searching for. Research on imagination suggests that if we are searching for something extraordinary - something that violates our intuitions about real-world causality - then we seek it in small doses and in contexts that ultimately confirm our intuitions. Imaginary worlds allow for true novelty, but we may actually prefer ideas that are novel on their surface but familiar at their core.

### Target Article 2: Menninghaus et al. (2017) - Negative Emotions in Art

_Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 40, e347: 1-63

- Main article: pp. 1-15
- Author's response: p. 58

**Recommended commentaries:**

Bullot, N. J., & Reber, R. (2017). Artistic misunderstandings: The emotional significance of historical learning in the arts. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _40_, e354. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X17001625

> _Abstract:_ The Distancing-Embracing model does not have the conceptual resources to explain artistic misunderstandings and the emotional consequences of historical learning in the arts. Specifically, it suggests implausible predictions about emotional distancing caused by art schemata (e.g., misunderstandings of artistic intentions and contexts). These problems show the need for further inquiries into how historical contextualization modulates negative emotions in the arts.

---

Davies, S. (2017). Distancing, not embracing, the Distancing-Embracing model of art reception. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _40_, e357. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X17001650

> _Abstract:_ Despite denials in the target article, the Distancing-Embracing model appeals to compensatory ideas in explaining the appeal of artworks that elicit negative affect. The model also appeals to the deflationary effects of psychological distancing. Having pointed to the famous rejection in the 1960s of the view that aesthetic experience involves psychological distancing, I suggest that "distance" functions here as a weak metaphor that cannot sustain the explanatory burden the theory demands of it.

---

Goldstein, T. R. (2017). Live theatre as exception and test case for experiencing negative emotions in art. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _40_, e362. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X17001704

> _Abstract:_ Distancing and then embracing constitutes a useful way of thinking about the paradox of aesthetic pleasure. However, the model does not account for live theatre. When live actors perform behaviors perceptually close to real life and possibly really experienced by the actors, audiences may experience autonomic reactions, with less distance, or may have to distance post-experiencing/embracing their emotions.

---

Nadal, M., Vartanian, O., & Skov, M. (2017). Psychological models of art reception must be empirically grounded. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _40_, e371. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X17001790

> _Abstract:_ We commend Menninghaus et al. for tackling the role of negative emotions in art reception. However, their model suffers from shortcomings that reduce its applicability to empirical studies of the arts: poor use of evidence, lack of integration with other models, and limited derivation of testable hypotheses. We argue that theories about art experiences should be based on empirical evidence.

---

Schubert, T. W., Seibt, B., Zickfeld, J. H., Blomster, J. K., & Fiske, A. P. (2017). Being moved is a positive emotion, and emotions should not be equated with their vernacular labels. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _40_, e374. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X17001820

> _Abstract:_ As evidence for the second process of the Embracing factor, the target article characterizes being moved as a mixed emotion linked to sadness through metonymy. We question these characterizations and argue that emotions should not be equated with their vernacular labels.

### Target Article 3: Savage et al. (2021) - Music as Social Bonding

_Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 44, e59: 1-77

- Main article: pp. 1-22
- Author's response: p. 61

**Recommended commentaries:**

Fritz, J. B. (2021). Is the MSB hypothesis (music as a coevolved system for social bonding) testable in the Popperian sense? _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e70. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001739

> _Abstract:_ "Music As a Coevolved System for Social Bonding" (MSB) is a brilliant synthesis and appealing hypothesis offering insights into the evolution and social bonding of musicality, but is so broad and sweeping it will be challenging to test, prove or falsify in the Popperian sense (Popper, 1959). After general comments, I focus my critique on underlying neurobiological mechanisms, and offer some suggestions for experimental tests of MSB.

---

Harrison, P. M. C., & Seale, M. (2021). Against unitary theories of music evolution. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e76. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001314

> _Abstract:_ Savage et al. and Mehr et al. provide well-substantiated arguments that the evolution of musicality was shaped by adaptive functions of social bonding and credible signalling. However, they are too quick to dismiss byproduct explanations of music evolution, and to present their theories as complete unitary accounts of the phenomenon.

**Note:** This commentary addresses both Savage and Mehr target articles and can be used for either.

---

Kraus, N., & Hesselmann, G. (2021). Musicality as a predictive process. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e81. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20000746

> _Abstract:_ Savage et al. argue for musicality as having evolved for the overarching purpose of social bonding. By way of contrast, we highlight contemporary predictive processing models of human cognitive functioning in which the production and enjoyment of music follows directly from the principle of prediction error minimization.

---

Merker, B. (2021). Music, bonding, and human evolution: A critique. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e83. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001429

> _Abstract:_ Savage et al. propose that music filled a hypothetical "bonding gap" in human sociality by Baldwinian gene-culture coevolution (or protracted cognitive niche construction). Both these stepping stones to an evolutionary account of the function and origin of music are problematic. They are scrutinized in this commentary, and an alternative is proposed.

_Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 44, e60: 1-87

- Main article: pp. 1-23
- Author's response: p. 69

**Recommended commentaries:**

Harrison, P. M. C., & Seale, M. (2021). Against unitary theories of music evolution. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e76. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001314

> _Abstract:_ Savage et al. and Mehr et al. provide well-substantiated arguments that the evolution of musicality was shaped by adaptive functions of social bonding and credible signalling. However, they are too quick to dismiss byproduct explanations of music evolution, and to present their theories as complete unitary accounts of the phenomenon.

**Note:** This commentary addresses both Savage and Mehr target articles and can be used for either.

Killin, A., Brusse, C., Currie, A., & Planer, R. J. (2021). Not by signalling alone: Music's mosaicism undermines the search for a proper function. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e103. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001107

> _Abstract:_ Mehr et al. seek to explain music's evolution in terms of a unitary proper function - signalling cooperative intent - which they cash out in two guises, coalition signalling and (allo)parental attention signalling. Although we recognize the role signalling almost certainly played in the evolution of music, we reject "ultimate" causal explanations which focus on a unidirectional, narrow range of causal factors.

---

Lieberman, D., & Billingsley, J. (2021). If it quacks like a duck: The by-product account of music still stands. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e106. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20000990

> _Abstract:_ Discerning adaptations from by-products is a defining feature of evolutionary science. Mehr, Krasnow, Bryant, and Hagen posit that music is an adaptation that evolved to function as a credible signal. We counter this claim, as we are not convinced they have dispelled the possibility that music is an elaboration of extant features of language.

---

Pinker, S. (2021). Sex and drugs and rock and roll. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e109. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001375

> _Abstract:_ This article is extraordinarily rigorous and rich, although there are reasons to be skeptical of its theory that music originated to signal group quality and infant solicitude. These include the lack of any signature of the centrality of these functions in the distribution or experience of music; of a role for the pleasure taken in music; and of its connections with language.

---

Stewart-Williams, S. (2021). Making music: Let's not be too quick to abandon the byproduct hypothesis. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, _44_, e113. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X20001119

> _Abstract:_ It is premature to conclude that music is an adaptation. Given the danger of overextending the adaptationist mode of explanation, the default position should be the byproduct hypothesis, and it should take very strong evidence to drag us into the adaptationist camp. As yet, the evidence isn't strong enough - and the proposed adaptationist explanations have a number of unresolved difficulties.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I change my target or commentary article?

See [About Article Selection](/course-ntw2029/assignments/papers/p2-2610/#about-article-selection) in the Paper 2 FAQ.

##### :x Why do I need both a paraphrase and a quotation?

Your paraphrase shows how you understand the passage, and the quotation lets both of us check that understanding against what the author actually wrote. The quotations also become the raw material for Part 4, where you check whether the commentary accurately represents what the target says; quoting in full sentences means you'll see the qualifiers the author actually used.

##### :x proposing an alternative commentary

If you'd like to use a commentary that isn't on the list, message me with its citation before you start on the questions. Look for one that identifies a specific problem with the target's argument. Strong criticisms typically claim the target does things like:

- Overlooks contradicting evidence
- Makes predictions that don't hold
- Defines key concepts too narrowly or broadly
- Can't explain important cases within its proposed scope
- Relies on flawed premises or reasoning

Avoid commentaries that merely offer alternative theories without showing problems in the target article.

##### :x criticism guidance

List the commentary's criticisms of the target, one line each, then identify the one you will analyze in the rest of this assignment. Sometimes criticisms overlap, but the goal here is to make sure you try to separate the different criticisms so that you have one narrow thing to focus on. If other criticisms become relevant later in the writing process, you can bring them back in.

For this question only, you can use a list of sentence fragments/phrases rather than full sentences

For example, an answer for Konečni's commentary on Menninghaus et al. (which you can't choose, since we discussed it in class) might look like this:

- Paradox of fiction: the target calls emotions felt toward fiction authentic, but readers' "sadness" for a fictional character isn't a genuine emotion
- Biology of emotions: genuine emotions respond to real threats and losses and are too costly to sustain, so readers don't actually feel them for fiction
- Music: "sad music" doesn't make listeners genuinely sad
- Distancing: if readers' sadness were genuine, distancing would be impossible, and if it isn't genuine, distancing is unnecessary
- Empirical support: the three studies the target cites have major methodological flaws and don't show genuine emotions

Focus: the paradox of fiction (emotions felt toward fiction aren't genuine)

##### :x question 2 guidance

To check for the criticism's significance, assume for the moment that the commentary is totally correct in its criticism. What would that mean for the target's argument? Would it completely undermine the target's argument? Limit its importance but not refute it? Or could the target authors say, "Yeah, your claim is totally correct," and carry on defending their article without changing it in any way?

If the criticism's impact is small enough that it wouldn't affect the target, consider picking another one.

##### :x question 3 guidance

First, paraphrase in one sentence how the commentary describes the target's position on the point being criticized.

- Don't describe your understanding of the target's position.
- Don't describe the commentary's criticism of the position.
- Do describe the target's position as the commentary presents it

Then, quote the passage where the commentary describes the target's position (1-3 sentences max), and label it C1.

##### :x question 4 guidance

In no more than three sentences, describe the criticism you chose in Question 1. First describe the criticism generally, at the level you'd use to tell a classmate what the disagreement is about. Then, use more specific language to explain the flaw, gap, or error the commentary says the target's argument has.

- Don't evaluate the criticism
- Don't worry about whether the commentary is right or wrong
- Do describe the criticism specifically

##### :x question 5 guidance

First, explain in no more than four sentences the reasons and/or evidence the commentary provides to support their claim about the target's position. A commentary often proposes its own explanation of the phenomenon the target aims to explain. If that account is the commentary's evidence, say what it shows about the target's claim, not what it says about the phenomenon.

- Don't restate the criticism
- Don't state the commentary's alternate theory
- Do explain the support the commentary provides for their criticism

Then, quote one or two passages that show the commentary's reasoning (1-3 sentences max for each), and label them C2, etc.

##### :x question 6 guidance

Quote two or three passages from the target article that discuss the thing the commentary criticizes, and label them T1, T2, and so on. Make one of them the passage where the target states the main claim the commentary criticizes. You don't need to paraphrase anything here.

In later questions, refer to these passages by label rather than quoting them again.

##### :x question 7 guidance

For each target passage, explain in no more than two sentences what the target is claiming there. Report only what the target says.

- Don't use the commentary's own terms for the target's position
- Don't yet say whether the commentary is right.
- Do explain what the target itself claims in each passage

##### :x question 8 guidance

Authors often qualify a claim somewhere other than where they first state it. Places to check include: the introduction, the place where the target defines its key terms, any section where the authors discuss limitations or answer possible criticisms, and the author's response.

Strategies for qualifying or weakening claims include:

- qualifying words, such as modal verbs (may, might, should), frequency terms (usually, typically, rarely), and scope markers (most, some, only)
- a statement that something lies outside the article's scope
- a definition that excludes particular cases
- a statement that the authors are not claiming something
- an admission that the evidence is incomplete, or that the claim is a hypothesis still to be tested

For a qualification stated in your Question 6 passages, give the passage label and the few words that qualify the claim. For a qualification found anywhere else, quote it and label it with the next T number.

Then, in no more than three sentences, say how strong the claim is once any qualifications are counted (how many cases it covers and how much it says about them) and how confident the authors are in it.

##### :x question 9 guidance

The target authors respond to the commentaries as a group. First, state whether their response addresses the criticism you chose to focus on in Question 1 (not some other criticism the same commentary makes).

If it does, quote the relevant passage or two and label them R1 and R2. Then paraphrase in no more than two sentences what the authors say.

If it does not, say so, and write no more than two sentences describing how you think the authors would respond, based on the target article. Label this as your own speculation.

##### :x how do i find the authors response

To find passages relevant to your specific commentary: (1) Open the file with the target and commentary articles, (2) Search (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F) for your commentary author's last name, (3) Read any passages in the author's response where they cite or discuss your commentary.

##### :x question 10 guidance

Imagine the target authors and the commentary authors in a room together, discussing the articles. The commentary authors say "You claim X," where X is the description you reported in Question 3. Using the target's own words from Question 6 and the qualifications from Question 8, say which of these responses the target authors would give:

- "Yes, we do claim X" (accurate characterization)
- "No, we actually claim Y" (mischaracterization)
- "Well, sort of, but not quite. We say something like that, but not exactly that." (over-reading)

Explain which answer fits and why, in no more than 150 words. Check specifically whether the commentary preserves the target's qualifiers, represents the target's scope accurately, strengthens or weakens the target's claim, or attributes a claim the target doesn't make. Refer to C1 and your target passages by label, quoting only the few words you are comparing.

##### :x question 11 guidance

If the authors responded to this criticism (Question 9), say in no more than four sentences whether their response confirms your Question 10 answer or complicates it. An author's response is itself a characterization, so check it the same way: does it describe the commentary's criticism accurately, or reply to something the commentary didn't say?

If the authors didn't respond, say in no more than two sentences whether the response you imagined in Question 9 fits your Question 10 answer.

##### :x question 12 guidance

Taking Questions 10 and 11 together, name the kind of finding in one sentence. Don't explain it again; your answers to Questions 10 and 11 already hold the explanation. Some common kinds are:

- **Match:** the commentary accurately represents the target's position
- **Over-reading:** the commentary strengthens the target's claim beyond what the target states (e.g. "X causes Y" becomes "only X causes Y")
- **Mischaracterization:** the commentary attributes a claim the target doesn't make
- **Target ambiguity:** the target's statements could mean more than one thing, and the commentary picks one reading
- **Terminological confusion:** the two articles use the same word with different meanings

If you used the authors' response, you may also have found **mutual misreading** (each article mischaracterizes the other) or **selective engagement** (the target replies to part of the criticism and ignores the rest). Or something else entirely. Don't force your case into these patterns; they are examples, not requirements.

##### :x question 13 guidance

Go back into the room. The target authors have now said what they actually claim (your Question 10 answer), and the commentary authors have heard it. Would the commentary authors still have an objection? In other words, would they now agree with the target article or not?

If the commentary's description of the target needed correcting (an over-reading, a mischaracterization, or another finding from Question 12), imagine the target authors making the correction. For an over-reading, that might be: "We didn't say always; we said typically." Would the commentary authors say "okay, never mind"? Or would they say "fine, but even typically is too strong, because..." and raise a different objection? If the second, that different objection is your real disagreement, and it is usually what the paper ends up being about.

If the commentary authors would still disagree, state in no more than three sentences what the two articles disagree about now that both agree on what was said, and explain why both positions cannot be true at the same time. This disagreement may sit somewhere other than where the commentary first put it.

If the commentary authors would now agree, say in no more than three sentences what the apparent disagreement was and why it dissolves once the target's actual claim is on the table. Say also whether anything smaller remains.

Answer this question regardless of what you answered for Question 12. Your answer here determines if the explicit disagreement is the actual disagreement, or if there is an impliict disagreement underneath the explicit disagreement.

##### :x question 14 guidance

In no more than three sentences, say what you are least certain about in your answers above: a passage you couldn't read with confidence, a qualifier you weren't sure how to weigh, a finding you nearly made differently. This is for me, so that my recorded feedback starts from your question rather than from a guess about it. The more specific you can be, the more helpful I can be. If you say something general, like , "I'm not sure about any of the above. I don't know if I'm reading it correctly," I can only give generic reassurance instead of actual help.
