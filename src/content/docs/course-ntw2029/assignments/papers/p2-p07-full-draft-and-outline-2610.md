## Purpose

This assignment helps you turn your work from P04-P06 into a complete argument, and the outline gives you a way to check that each paragraph advances your thesis before you write the final paper.

## Overview

Write a complete draft of Paper 2, up to 2750 words max, analyzing the disagreement between your target article and commentary, integrating insights and feedback from your peer reviews. Along with the draft, include the other parts listed under Requirements (don't include them in your word count, which is for the draft only).

## Requirements

Write one document with several parts. Start each part on a new page, but keep the parts as one document in one Google Docs tab.

1. **Full draft**: Write a complete essay with an introduction that proposes your thesis, a body that explains and analyzes the disagreement, and a conclusion that discusses the significance of your thesis. [:What to include in each part of your draft](#x-what-to-include-in-each-part-of-your-draft) Cite at least 4 academic sources in the text of your essay. [:Using additional sources appropriately](#x-using-additional-sources-appropriately)

2. **Reference list**: Include a reference list after your draft, following the guidelines on the Citations page in the Resources section.

3. **Simplified outline**: On a new page after your reference list, list your thesis, the topic sentence of every body paragraph in order, and a 1-2 sentence significance statement taken from your conclusion. Download an [example outline](/downloads/paper2-draft-outline-example.pdf). [:How to create and use your outline](#x-how-to-create-and-use-your-outline)

4. **Reflection**: After your outline, briefly describe: (a) the key terms or concepts from the articles that are most important for your argument, (b) aspects you're having trouble with or feel uncertain about, and/or (c) any specific questions you have about your draft.

## Feedback

I'll provide recorded feedback on P07, starting with your argument as presented in the outline, and we'll discuss it in a conference during Week 12.

##### :x what to include in each part of your draft

- **Introduction**: In your introduction:

	- introduce your reader to the academic conversation and the phenomenon the target article aims to explain
	- convey the target article's thesis or main conclusion about the phenomenon (briefly, but in a way a generalist reader can understand)
	- convey the commentary's criticism (briefly, but in a way a generalist reader can understand)
	- propose your thesis about what drives the disagreement
	- after the thesis, preview the significance of your thesis in 1-2 sentences
- **Body**: Explain the target article's argument, the commentary's criticism, and the author's response if it addresses the criticism. Analyze the disagreement to support your thesis.

- **Conclusion**: Discuss the significance of your thesis, meaning what your analysis shows beyond the specific disagreement your paper focuses on. [:Implications and significance](#x-implications-and-significance)

- **Sources**: The target article, commentary, and author's response each count as one source. Cite every source in the text of your essay; a source that appears only in your reference list doesn't count.

##### :x implications and significance

In this assignment, I use two terms for what you learn by analyzing the specific disagreement your paper focuses on:

- "Implications" refers to consequences relevant just to that specific disagreement, such as what it would take to settle the dispute, what other ideas each position would have to agree with, or what a concept would have to mean to be consistent with a claim. Implications belong in the body of your paper.
- "Significance" refers to consequences relevant outside that specific disagreement, such as consequences for the broader academic conversation on the topic, for how researchers in the field approach issues, or for questions outside the topic. Significance belongs in your conclusion.

##### :x using additional sources appropriately

**Appropriate uses:**

- Provide background on concepts or theories mentioned in articles
- Verify or evaluate specific empirical claims, such as whether a study the commentary cites says what the commentary claims
- Understand technical terms or disciplinary context
- Support your reading of the target and commentary using other sources by the same authors
- Fill a gap identified by your analysis of the disagreement
- Use a source to clarify or complicate your analysis
- Bring in other commentaries on the same issue that bear on your analysis

Whatever the use, make sure to:

- keep the focus on analyzing the disagreement between the target and the commentary
- connect each source explicitly to the target's and commentary's claims, concepts, or arguments

##### :x how to create and use your outline

**Format:**

- Start with your thesis statement (not numbered).
- Then list your body paragraph topic sentences in a flat numbered list in order of appearance (no section headers, no sub-numbering), including every topic sentence.
- At the end, add your significance statement, 1-2 sentences from your conclusion that capture why your argument matters beyond the specific disagreement you analyzed.
- You don't need to highlight the sentences in the outline.

**Using the outline as you write:**

- Most essays have 10-15 body paragraphs. If you have more or fewer, check that your paragraphs follow the rule "one idea per paragraph."
- If your topic sentence doesn't capture the main idea of the paragraph, the mismatch tells you that either the topic sentence or the paragraph needs revision. Revise the topic sentence in your draft so it captures what the paragraph is actually about, or revise the paragraph so it fits the topic sentence, then put the revised topic sentence in the outline. The outline should represent the point of each paragraph, not just announce what it will discuss (see [announcing writing moves instead of stating the main point](/course-ntw2029/resources/topic-sentences/#announcing-writing-moves-instead-of-stating-the-main-point)).
- Keep the outline updated as you write your draft, and read through it regularly to check that your argument flows logically and that each paragraph advances your thesis. I suggest going back and forth between the draft and the outline as a way of keeping the big picture in mind.
- If you apply the writing guidance taught throughout the course (one idea per paragraph, topic sentences start paragraphs, topic sentences capture the full idea of each paragraph), the outline will fairly represent your essay's argument.
