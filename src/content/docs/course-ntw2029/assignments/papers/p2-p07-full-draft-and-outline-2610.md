## Purpose

This assignment helps you turn your work from P04-P06 into a complete argument, and the outline gives you a way to check that each paragraph advances your thesis before you write the final paper.

## Overview

Write a complete draft of Paper 2, up to 2750 words max, analyzing the disagreement between your target article and commentary, integrating insights and feedback from your peer reviews. Along with the draft, include the other parts listed under Requirements (don't include them in your word count, which is for the draft only).

## Requirements

Write one document with several parts. Start each part on a new page, but keep the parts as one document in one Google Docs tab.

1. **Full draft**: Write a complete essay with an introduction that proposes your thesis, a body that explains and analyzes the disagreement, and a conclusion that discusses the significance of your thesis. ## Guidelines

### Introduction

Keep your introduction's details to what your reader needs to follow the paper. Your reader needs orientation to the specific criticism you're analyzing, not a comprehensive explanation of the articles' positions. Your introduction to the puzzle can be shorter than in Paper 1.

Take care to introduce new ideas and concepts in one sentence before combining them with other concepts in a later sentence.

### Thesis

Your working thesis from the last assignment identified what drives the disagreement. Your draft thesis should reflect what your continued analysis revealed, which might mean:

- refining that identification
- explaining why the difference exists
	- e.g. what theoretical goals or disciplinary contexts lead each side to its position?
- replacing that identification if your analysis showed that something else drives the disagreement (e.g. you thought the two used different definitions, but they turn out to apply different standards of evidence)
- showing that the two positions in your implicit disagreement aren't mutually exclusive, if your analysis found that both can be true at once (e.g. one explains how a trait works and the other explains why it evolved)
	- e.g. do the two sides answer different research questions, or different parts of one question?
- combining the two theories, if the two sides use a key term in different senses and their theories can be combined once the senses are separated
- checking a study the commentary cites, to see whether it says what the commentary claims

<!-- - evaluating the positions, once you've explained why the two disagree -->

- working with a concession, if the target's response grants the commentary's point: what was conceded, why the gap exists, and what addressing it would take

Your draft thesis doesn't have to have Parts A, B, and C in the style of the working thesis. It may omit parts unimportant to your argument or add new types of observations that came out of your analysis, and parts you leave out of the thesis can still appear in the body as steps in your analysis.

If your verification found a problem with the commentary's description of the target, such as mischaracterization or terminological confusion, ask yourself whether understanding that problem is important for understanding the disagreement. If it isn't, omit it from the paper. If it is, state the problem in your thesis. You don't have to use the labels from the verification assignment; use terms your generalist reader will understand.

### Body

In the body, explain the two positions on the criticism you chose: the part of the target's argument the criticism addresses, the commentary's criticism, and the author's response if it addresses the criticism. Explain them more fully than the introduction's brief characterization, but leave out parts of either article the criticism doesn't concern. I usually recommend giving the explanation first, then the analysis, but in some essays mixing explanation and analysis as you move from topic to topic works better.

**Explaining the two positions**

Use these three habits to make your explanation easier to follow:

- Set up the two positions in one sentence before you discuss either one.
- State a claim before its reason.
- Signal how each sentence relates to the one before it (e.g. with "but," "because," or "for example").

Every claim about what an author holds should rest on a passage in the text:

- Quote or cite the passage for each position you attribute to an author.
- When you infer an assumption the author never states, say that the inference is yours and give the passages it rests on.
- Describe what the author says rather than applying a label another author uses (e.g., don't call the target's position "the byproduct view" if the target never uses that term, just because the commentary does).

Let your verification findings shape how you explain the positions:

- If your verification found that the commentary describes the target accurately, you may state the commentary's claims about the target without discussing verification at length.
- If it found a problem that matters to the disagreement (see the Introduction guidelines), first present the commentary's claim as the commentary states it, so your reader knows what was claimed, then quote the target to show what it actually says.

**Key terms**

Some terms carry the disagreement (e.g. credible signal, domain-general, musicality versus music). Before you explain one, work out how each author uses it:

- Look for a passage where the author defines the term. If there isn't one, collect several passages where the author uses it, and note what the author applies it to and what the author contrasts it with.
- Write the meaning in your own words and check it against those passages.
- Use a background source to learn how the field usually uses the term (the "Using additional sources appropriately" link under the Full draft requirement lists this as an appropriate use), but rely on the author's own passages for the author's sense, since an author may use a term in a narrower or different sense.
- If you're still unsure, name the term in your reflection so we can discuss it in the conference.

If the target and the commentary use a term differently, the difference isn't a reading mistake on your part. It may be what drives the disagreement, or a problem with the commentary's description of the target. Explain each author's sense separately, and make clear which sense you mean each time you use the term.

Then explain each of those terms in your paper for a reader who hasn't read the articles. Two ways to check that you have:

- Ask someone who hasn't read the articles to read your explanation and tell you where they lost the thread.
- Read your explanation looking only for terms you use before you define them.

**Analyzing the disagreement**

If your thesis says that a difference between the two authors drives their disagreement, the analysis is where you support that claim. Show how each author's position on that difference leads to what they explicitly say about the criticism, with quotations from both articles. The explanation you wrote in the last assignment is a first draft of this part of the body. Without it, your reader has only your word that the difference drives the disagreement.

Keep the paper on the criticism you chose. Your paper should analyze what underlies the disagreement over that criticism, not whether the commentary's alternative theory is true. Include side issues the articles raise only if they bear on that disagreement. For the same reason, use an outside theory when it builds on your explanation of why the two disagree, e.g. by filling a gap that explanation exposed (see the Weisberg & Friend example under "The stretch goal"), and not to replace that explanation with a verdict on which side is right.

I recommend working out at least one implication of your thesis in the body rather than only naming it. An implication is a consequence of your thesis for the specific disagreement you analyze, such as what it would take to settle the dispute. Version 3 of the example theses under Assessment guidelines (in "The stretch goal") shows what working out an implication looks like.

### Conclusion

Your significance discussion answers one question. If your thesis is true, what effect would that have outside the specific disagreement your paper focuses on? In other words, how would your claim be meaningful outside the narrow scope of the one commentary criticism you discuss in your paper?

To answer that question, ask if the main idea of your thesis might matter:

- in the broader academic conversation on the topic, e.g. does it explain why a larger debate persists, or show something that conversation has missed?
- in how researchers approach the topic, e.g. does it show something researchers would need to agree on, define, or do differently for the larger debate to move forward?
- in questions outside the topic, e.g. could it help explain a debate about a different phenomenon?

Worked example for significance:

Verpooten & Eens criticize Savage et al.'s music and social bonding (MSB) hypothesis.

The MSB hypothesis predicts that more musical species should have more complex social bonds.

Verpooten & Eens divide animal vocalizations into learned contact calls and songs, and say that learned contact calls aren't music-like, while songs are.

Verpooten & Eens say that learned contact calls correlate with complex social bonds, but songs don't, and conclude that the evidence goes against the MSB hypothesis.

Savage et al. reply that, by Fitch's definition, learned contact calls are songs, and thus musical, so the evidence supports MSB's prediction.

A student paper argues that:

the explicit disagreement is whether the evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis

the implicit disagreement underlying the explicit disagreement is about which animal vocalizations should count as musical

Thesis: _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence shows that more musical species have more complex social bonds because they differ on which animal vocalizations count as musical. Verpooten & Eens evaluate vocalizations by their structure, and thus state that learned contact calls aren't musical, while Savage et al. evaluate them by the vocal learning behind them, and hold that learned contact calls are also songs._

Significance that doesn't explain how this thesis affects issues outside the disagreement:

_This disagreement matters because the evolution of music is an important question._

That statement would be true whatever the student's thesis said.

Significance that does explain how this thesis affects issues outside the disagreement:

_Whether researchers count an animal vocalization as musical by its structure or by the vocal learning behind it affects the usefulness of cross-species evidence in research on music's evolutionary origins. Comparisons that sort vocalizations by structure, into songs and calls, test a different idea of music than theories that define musicality by vocal learning, such as Savage et al.'s. Cross-species comparisons can test such theories only if researchers first agree on which criterion to use._

That statement uses the thesis's distinction between structure and vocal learning and says what the distinction means for research outside this one disagreement.

In your draft, aim for significance like the second statement. In the conference, if your thesis supports even broader significance, I'll encourage you to develop it further.

I ask for only a brief preview in the introduction because readers expect to find the answer to "so what does this mean?" in the conclusion.

### Assessment guidelines

These guidelines describe what your final Paper 2 should aim for. I describe quality with two reference points: an expectation that every paper should meet and a stretch goal that stronger papers reach toward. The two reference points mark positions on a spectrum rather than boxes, and how well your paper carries out each part places it closer to or further from each point.

You will successfully reach the stretch goal only by focusing on meeting the expectation, since whatever you add has to grow out of your analysis of this disagreement rather than come from outside it. Students who try to reach the stretch goal directly typically fall short on both. If you are solidly meeting the expectation, in the conference, I'll suggest ways your paper can go further.

**The expectation**

The expectation is an accurate thesis about what drives the disagreement, built from Parts B and C of your last assignment (and Part A where the Thesis guidelines call for it), plus a significance discussion.

Meeting the expectation is an achievement in itself. Some disagreements are hard to explain clearly, and a paper that does so is valuable even if its thesis seems unsurprising to you.

A thesis can go wrong in several ways. The examples below use Verpooten & Eens's commentary on Savage et al., [:the third example in the last assignment](/course-ntw2029/assignments/papers/p2-p06-going-beneath-2610/#x-three-examples).

- Avoid: restating the explicit disagreement in new words
	- e.g. _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they interpret that evidence differently. Verpooten & Eens think it shows the prediction fails, and Savage et al. think it doesn't._
	- The "because" clause and the second sentence restate Part B, what each side thinks the evidence shows, instead of naming a difference that explains Part B.
	- To test whether your Part C goes beneath Part B, ask:
		- Is there a fact, case, or factor that one side treats as important and the other doesn't? If so, why does it matter to one side and not the other?
		- What makes each side judge the evidence "enough"?
		- If the two sides agreed on your Part C, would they still disagree about Part B? If they would, your Part C doesn't explain Part B.
		- If the commentary says the target fails to show something, does the target think its argument has to show it?
- Avoid: misreading the sources
	- e.g. _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they differ on what that evidence covers. Savage et al. reply that the learned contact calls in Verpooten & Eens's review aren't music, so the evidence doesn't bear on their prediction._
	- The second sentence misreads the target. Savage et al. say the reverse: by Fitch's definition, learned contact calls are "songs," so their correlation with social complexity supports the prediction rather than undermining it.
- Avoid: taking a side
	- e.g. _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they differ on which animal vocalizations count as musical. Since only song-like vocalizations are music-like, Verpooten & Eens show that Savage et al.'s prediction fails._
	- The first sentence names a difference that explains the disagreement, but the second argues who's right instead of explaining why the two disagree.
- Avoid: proposing a fix before correctly identifying what drives the disagreement
	- e.g. _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because that evidence covers too few species, so the disagreement would be settled if researchers studied more species._
	- The "because" clause names the wrong cause, and the fix follows from it. Data on more species wouldn't settle the disagreement, since the target and commentary would still disagree about which vocalizations count as musical.

**The stretch goal**

The stretch goal is to add more to the academic conversation than your working thesis does. Part C is the beginning of your contribution, but don't assume that your contribution is limited to Part C.

Any of the ways your draft thesis can develop reaches toward the stretch goal when it produces a new idea that neither article offers, such as a framework, a distinction, or a new way of thinking about the debate. Making an implicit idea explicit, as Part C does, meets the expectation, and so does one of those moves made in a more basic way. For example, a thesis saying that two positions which seem opposed are actually compatible might resolve an ambiguity without adding much to the conversation, while one showing a new way the two fit together might add to the conversation.

The same test applies to your significance: does it say something the field doesn't already know? See "A thesis that meets the expectation" under the "Examples of thesis and significance" link below.

How far you develop the implications of your thesis in the body (see the Body guidelines) is one of the main places papers differ, since a strong paper works an implication out until it reaches a new idea that neither article offers.

For example, consider a past student's paper on Weisberg & Friend's commentary on Menninghaus et al. (the first example in the last assignment):

- The paper showed that the commentary misreads the target.
- The paper then argued that the misreading points to a real gap, since the DEM doesn't adequately explain how non-fiction creates distance in a way that allows its negative effects to result in positive emotions.
- The paper filled the gap with an outside source, von Engelhardt & Jansz's theory of how viewers keep their distance from real suffering they see in the media, and showed how that theory meets the DEM's conditions for distancing.

Another example is based on a working thesis on Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al., followed by two draft theses that develop it in different ways:

1. The working thesis (a short version of the last assignment's example thesis): _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they differ on which animal vocalizations count as musical._

2. A draft thesis that refines the working thesis, stating the difference precisely for both terms of the prediction: _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they define and measure both terms of the prediction differently. Verpooten & Eens identify musical behavior by its structure (long, complex, ordered, repeated song), while Savage et al. count learned contact calls as songs and compare acoustic complexity within each type of vocalization. Verpooten & Eens identify social complexity by complex group life, such as elephants' fission-fusion societies, while Savage et al. say monogamy and joint parental care also make heavy social demands. So the two are comparing the evidence to different predictions._

3. A draft thesis that also works out an implication of the working thesis: _Verpooten & Eens and Savage et al. disagree about whether the cross-species evidence contradicts the MSB hypothesis because they differ on which animal vocalizations count as musical. Savage et al.'s broader definition of musical behavior protects their prediction but makes it hard to test, since almost any learned vocalization in a socially complex species can count as musical._

Version 1 meets the expectation by naming the difference that drives the disagreement. Version 2 is stronger because it also states that difference precisely for both terms of the prediction, showing exactly where the two positions part ways, making an implicit difference explicit. Version 3 is stronger still because it also works out an implication of that difference until it reaches a new idea that neither article offers (that Savage et al.'s broader definition makes their prediction hard to test), so it reaches farther toward the stretch goal.

## Feedback

##### :x what to include in each part of your draft

- **Introduction**: In your introduction:

	- introduce your reader to the academic conversation and the phenomenon the target article aims to explain
	- convey the target article's thesis or main conclusion about the phenomenon (briefly, but in a way a generalist reader can understand)
	- convey the commentary's criticism (briefly, but in a way a generalist reader can understand)
	- propose your thesis about what drives the disagreement
	- after the thesis, preview the significance of your thesis in 1-2 sentences
- **Body**: Explain the part of the target article's argument that the criticism addresses, the commentary's criticism, and the author's response if it addresses the criticism. Leave out parts of either article the criticism doesn't concern. Analyze the disagreement to support your thesis.

- **Conclusion**: Discuss the significance of your thesis, meaning what your analysis shows beyond the specific disagreement your paper focuses on. [:Implications and significance](#x-implications-and-significance)

- **Sources**: The target article, commentary, and author's response each count as one source. Cite every source in the text of your essay; a source that appears only in your reference list doesn't count.

##### :x implications and significance

In this assignment, I use two terms for what you learn by analyzing the specific disagreement your paper focuses on:

- "Implications" refers to consequences relevant just to that specific disagreement, such as what it would take to settle the dispute, what other ideas each position would have to agree with, or what a concept would have to mean to be consistent with a claim. Implications belong in the body of your paper.
- "Significance" refers to consequences relevant outside that specific disagreement, such as consequences for the broader academic conversation on the topic, for how researchers in the field approach issues, or for questions outside the topic. Significance belongs in your conclusion.

##### :x using additional sources appropriately

**Appropriate uses:**

- Provide background on concepts or theories mentioned in articles
- Verify or evaluate specific empirical claims, such as whether a study the commentary cites says what the commentary claims
- Understand technical terms or disciplinary context
- Support your reading of the target and commentary using other sources by the same authors
- Fill a gap identified by your analysis of the disagreement
- Use a source to clarify or complicate your analysis
- Bring in other commentaries on the same issue that bear on your analysis

Whatever the use, make sure to:

- keep the focus on analyzing the disagreement between the target and the commentary
- connect each source explicitly to the target's and commentary's claims, concepts, or arguments

##### :x how to create and use your outline

**Format:**

- Start with your thesis statement (not numbered).
- Then list your body paragraph topic sentences in a flat numbered list in order of appearance (no section headers, no sub-numbering), including every topic sentence.
- At the end, add your significance statement, 1-2 sentences from your conclusion that capture why your argument matters beyond the specific disagreement you analyzed.
- You don't need to highlight the sentences in the outline.

**Using the outline as you write:**

- Most essays have 10-15 body paragraphs. If you have more or fewer, check that your paragraphs follow the rule "one idea per paragraph."
- If your topic sentence doesn't capture the main idea of the paragraph, the mismatch tells you that either the topic sentence or the paragraph needs revision. Revise the topic sentence in your draft so it captures what the paragraph is actually about, or revise the paragraph so it fits the topic sentence, then put the revised topic sentence in the outline. The outline should represent the point of each paragraph, not just announce what it will discuss (see [announcing writing moves instead of stating the main point](/course-ntw2029/resources/topic-sentences/#announcing-writing-moves-instead-of-stating-the-main-point)).
- Keep the outline updated as you write your draft, and read through it regularly to check that your argument flows logically and that each paragraph advances your thesis. I suggest going back and forth between the draft and the outline as a way of keeping the big picture in mind.
- If you apply the writing guidance taught throughout the course (one idea per paragraph, topic sentences start paragraphs, topic sentences capture the full idea of each paragraph), the outline will fairly represent your essay's argument.

##### :x examples of thesis and significance

**A thesis that falls short of the expectation:**

- Thesis: _They disagree about whether photography qualifies as art; the target argues photography is art, the commentary argues photography is merely mechanical reproduction._
- Significance: _Understanding this disagreement is important because photography is widely used and resolving whether it's art matters for the field._
- Why this falls short: This thesis just identifies opposing positions without explaining what drives them, so it doesn't add much to the academic conversation. Thus, the significance claim is limited to stating that the topic is important, which would be true whatever the thesis said.

**A thesis that meets the expectation:**

- Thesis: _They disagree about photography's status as art because they rely on different definitions of art. The target defines art by aesthetic response while the commentary defines it as an act of creative intention._
- Significance: _Understanding this definitional disagreement reveals that resolving the debate requires prior agreement on whether art is defined by production or reception, not more analysis of photography's features._
- Why this meets the expectation: The thesis identifies the difference in definitions that drives the disagreement (a Part C), and the significance says what that difference means for the larger debate. It stops at the expectation because the field already knows that definitions of art differ. To reach farther towards the stretch goal, the paper would need a new idea that neither article offers, for example an argument, made by neither author, about why one definition serves the debate better than the other.

**A thesis that reaches toward the stretch goal:**

- Thesis: _The commentary characterizes the target as claiming photographers "create" images, but the target's actual claim is that photographers "select and frame" existing visual arrangements. This disconnect reveals that the ordinary concept of "artistic creation" conflates two distinct activities: generating new configurations versus recognizing and isolating existing ones._
- Significance: _Understanding this conceptual ambiguity reveals why debates about photography's artistic status persist despite extensive discussion: the term "creation" carries unexamined assumptions about what artistic activity involves. This pattern extends to other selection-based practices (found art, readymades, curatorial work), suggesting that progress on this topic requires more specification of the ordinary-language concept of "creating art."_
- Why this reaches toward the stretch goal: The thesis works the commentary's misreading out into a new idea that neither article offers: that the ordinary concept of "artistic creation" conflates two activities. The significance carries that idea beyond the dispute between these two articles to other selection-based practices.
